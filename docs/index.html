Methods

All results were generated from synthetic respondents. These are not survey results. The analysis was carried out in Python (version [X]) (Python Software Foundation, n.d.), using the packages pandas (McKinney 2010), NumPy (Harris et al. 2020), pyarrow (Apache Arrow Developers, n.d.), requests (Reitz, n.d.), matplotlib (Hunter 2007), and sentence-transformers (Reimers and Gurevych 2019). This page was built with Quarto (Allaire et al. 2022).

Data

To generate realistic digital twins, we first obtained respondent records from the 2024 Cooperative Election Study (CES), which includes 60,000 US adults (Ansolabehere et al. 2025). Of these respondents, 38,307 were in one of the 35 states with a 2026 Senate race (33 regular elections and 2 special elections), and 23,025 of that subset were assigned a weight (“vvweight_post”), which we used as the probability of being drawn when sampling personas within each state. According to the 2024 CES Guide, “vvweight_post” is recommended for research that characterizes the opinions, behaviours, or traits of voters, where “vv” stands for “voter validated” (Ansolabehere et al. 2025). The “post” designation indicates that the respondent is not only matched to a registration record but also completed both the pre-election and post-election waves of the study. We relied on “vvweight_post” because we were interested in voter behaviour, and because the features we chose to construct our personas were taken from both the pre- and post-election questionnaires.

A known limitation of LLM synthetic voting is partisan bias that depends on the model (Murray and Alexander 2026; Chapekis et al. 2026). As a remedy, synthetic votes would be weighted with polling data obtained from PollResults, an open polling data platform that compiles and aggregates polls originally tracked by The New York Times pertaining to the Senate races (PollResults.org 2026). News updates were summarized and taken from Google News with a focus on the following sources, which span the political spectrum: The New York Times, Washington Post, The Wall Street Journal, USA Today, Fox News, New York Post, The Daily Wire, HuffPost, Mother Jones, and Vox. Sources were selected based on both their viewership [CITATION NEEDED: viewership source] and their political leaning.

Personas

We built each persona from seven variables: age, education, race, gender, household income, ideology, and party identification. Age was calculated by subtracting each respondent’s birth year from 2024 and then sorting the result into four brackets: 18-29, 30-44, 45-64, and 65+. Education had six options: not finishing high school, a high school diploma, some college without a degree, a 2-year college degree, a 4-year college degree, and a post-graduate degree. Race was recorded as “White”, “Black”, “Hispanic”, “Asian”, or “Other”. Gender was recorded as “man”, “woman”, “non-binary person”, or “other”. Household income had 16 brackets, from less than $10,000 a year to $500,000 or more, plus a “Prefer not to say” option. Ideology had five options, “Very liberal”, “Liberal”, “Moderate”, “Conservative”, and “Very conservative”, plus “Not sure”. Party identification had seven options, “Strong Democrat”, “Weak Democrat”, “Lean Democrat”, “Independent”, “Lean Republican”, “Weak Republican”, and “Strong Republican”, plus “Not sure”.

Mode Collapse

An additional limitation of LLM synthetic voting is mode collapse. This is where an LLM generates responses that concentrate around the mode of the true distribution, which results in synthetic distributions with unrealistically low variance. The consequences include overconfident estimates and inaccurate power calculations (Heath and Alexander 2026). As such, we used Semantic Similarity Rating (SSR).

The anchors for SSR were adapted from Heath and Alexander (2026), with statements about vote choice in place of favourability. The five statements are “I will definitely vote for (left)”, “I lean toward voting for (left)”, “I am undecided, I will vote for someone else, or I will not vote”, “I lean toward voting for (right)”, and “I will definitely vote for (right)”.

For each persona’s response, we calculated the cosine similarity between the response embedding and each embedded anchor point, which gave a list of five similarities per response (Heath and Alexander 2026). Following Heath and Alexander (2026), we applied min-max normalization to scale the similarities to a 0 to 1 range, followed by a softmax function to turn the normalized similarities into probabilities. However, there are some differences from Heath and Alexander. Their digital twins rate their feelings toward four groups on a 0 to 100 thermometer, whereas ours are asked for their vote choice in a Senate race, so our five statements were modified for candidate choice rather than favourability. In addition, Heath and Alexander put the weights on the five anchors into a Gaussian kernel density estimate, which gives a smooth distribution over 0 to 100. We instead sum the softmax weights into three numbers, p_left, p_rep, and p_other, with no smoothing. We use a temperature of 0.25 and do not fit it on our own data, because we have no individual-level truth to fit against. Currently, Gemini Embedding 2 is the model that we use, but we use it symmetrically, whereas Heath and Alexander use it asymmetrically (confirm with group if we will change it).

Something to note is that SSR was developed for ratings on an ordered scale from 0 to 100 (Heath and Alexander 2026). Vote choice is not an ordered scale, and our middle anchor is a separate category and not a neutral point, so applying SSR to vote choice is our own adaptation.

Sampling

Sampling was conducted by state, and 100 personas were sampled per state. Some states had fewer than 100 respondents with a weight in the 2024 CES data. These were Wyoming (54 of 95 respondents with a weight) and Alaska (88 of 128 respondents with a weight). For consistency, sampling with replacement was used across all 35 states.

Prompts and models

Each persona was asked how it would vote in its state’s Senate race. Following Murray and Alexander (2026), we query 4 different models, as different LLMs have different biases. We also save model_returned and the query times, because Bisbee et al. (2024) state that the model behind a name can change even when the name stays the same. Candidate order was randomised for each persona.

A second design, Design C, asked the persona to think of 100 people in its state who share its background and views, and to say how many of those 100 would vote for each of the two main candidates, how many for another candidate, and how many would not vote. The four numbers they give must add up to 100, and the full wording is in the AI Prompts section. We implemented this design because Heath and Alexander compare SSR against a numeric prompt on a 0 to 100 scale (Heath and Alexander 2026). Since Senate vote choice is divided into distinct categories, asking for numbers out of 100 gives us a numerical scale, which lets us compare Design B (free text scored with SSR) with a direct numeric design in the same way.

Turning Answers into Voting Probabilities

For Design B, we used SSR (Maier et al. 2025; Heath and Alexander 2026). We embedded each answer and the five statements written for that race, from “I will definitely vote for [Democrat]” to “I will definitely vote for [Republican]”, with an undecided statement in the middle. We took the cosine similarity between the answer and each statement, rescaled the five values to 0 to 1, and applied a softmax with a temperature of 0.25. The two statements on each side were summed, which gives p(left), p(Republican), and p(other) for each answer. Heath and Alexander tested SSR on 0 to 100 ratings, so using it for vote choice is our adaptation. We used [the local all-MiniLM-L6-v2 model / Gemini Embedding 2], [which differs from their asymmetric Gemini setup]. For Design C, we parsed the percentages and rescaled totals between 90 and 110 to 100. Answers that were empty, refused, or could not be parsed were counted and left out.

Testing and Checks

As a test run, we used Poolside’s Laguna (poolside/laguna-s-2.1:free) and Nvidia’s Nemotron 3 Ultra model (nvidia/nemotron-3-ultra-550b-a55b:free). Five personas were drawn from each state with repeated sampling, and SSR embedding was conducted using a local embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2).

We also ran a simulation to understand how our final dataset would be constructed, with tests to evaluate its quality and legitimacy. For the candidates, we checked that each of the 35 races had 2 main candidates. For the personas, we checked that all labels and group designations followed the master list of options for each demographic category, that each state had the same number of personas with one ID each, that a repeated CES respondent stayed in one state and had identical answers each time, that there were no missing values, and that the 2024 presidential vote was not in the persona text. For the responses, we checked that the share of empty or failed answers per model was below the limit of 0.10, that every prompt was answered once per model and repeat, that there was one row per persona and design, and that each prompt named both candidates of its race. For the scores, we checked that probabilities were between 0 and 1 and added up to 1 where they existed, that only rows with status “ok” had probabilities and every row had a status, and that every state had at least one scored persona for every model (Design B).

For the test forecast, we checked that each race, draw, and model had one winner and that it was the party with the larger share. We also checked that there were no missing shares, that all shares were between 0 and 1 and added up to 1, that the winner was a party running in that race (the left party of the state, or Republican), that every draw filled 100 seats, and that every state and model had the same number of draws.

Discussion

Limitations

Because we used real polling data to correct for biased LLM responses via weighted averages, the forecast is no longer an independent test of the models. For states that had no real polling data available, the “average gap” was applied. However, this assumes that the model’s lean is the same everywhere, when some states may lean more Democratic or more Republican. The gap between the synthetic vote distributions and the polling data is ultimately measured against polls, and polls have their own error, which may be correlated across states (Shirani-Mehr et al. 2018). However, we hoped to mitigate this by relying on a poll aggregator (PollResults).

In order to apply SSR, we had to flatten the candidate choices in each state to 2 sides to create a scale from Democrat to Republican, or from Independent to Republican, depending on which party the candidates belonged to. As a result, minor candidates and “don’t know” answers are not fully reflected in these results.

Due to cost, we could not update the news summaries every day. Instead, news summaries were provided and the models were re-run on October 27th, 29th, and 31st. Murray and Alexander (2026) found that adding party identification to persona descriptions ultimately reduced the impact that news had on synthetic voting results. Further research could examine how different news diets, or differing amounts of news, affect synthetic voting results.

Our original plan was to take data from multiple pollsters and divide them into a training set and a validation set. We would have used the training set to correct for bias in our model, and then compared the corrected results against aggregated results from the validation set. However, due to time constraints, we were unable to use this approach. We also have no real data to validate our model against, as the 2026 midterms have not yet occurred.

Furthermore, several limitations arise from applying SSR, which Heath and Alexander applied to a scale of 0 to 100 ratings, to vote choice (Heath and Alexander 2026). There is no fitted temperature, whereas Heath and Alexander fit theirs on their own data. Vote choice is not a scale, and our middle anchor is a separate category. We keep only the left, Republican, and other weights, which loses detail but fits a two-sided forecast. SSR is meant to address the mode collapse problem, and we can test this by comparing Design B with Design C.
